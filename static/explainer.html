Why this project exists

The problem you signed up to solve

Electricity reaches your building through a substation: a fenced yard or room full of switchgear that takes high-voltage power from the grid and steps it down for local use. Every substation has protective devices whose only job is to notice a fault and disconnect it fast.

Those devices need settings — how much current counts as a fault, how long to wait before acting. Today a human engineer calculates those settings by hand, in a spreadsheet, over days. They are then written into the relays and, in practice, left untouched for years.

Two things go wrong because of that.

Wrong settings cause blackouts. If the settings are badly chosen, a small fault on one street trips the breaker for the whole neighbourhood. One fault becomes a large outage.

Nobody knows the real danger level. When a fault arcs inside switchgear, it releases a burst of heat that can kill a technician standing in front of it. How bad it would be is calculated once, printed on a sticker, and stuck on the panel door. That sticker is often five years old and no longer true.

Your problem statement, decoded

"Substations rely on static, manual relay calculations and periodic safety audits" means the settings are stale and the safety label is stale. Everything in this project attacks those two staleness problems — and, it turns out, they are the same problem.

Three things to understand first

Everything else follows from these

1. A relay is a smart fuse

Think of the fuse box in a house. If a lamp shorts out, you want the lamp's fuse to blow — not the main fuse for the whole house. If the main one goes first you lose every room instead of one lamp.

A substation has the same arrangement, much bigger. A relay watches the current flowing through one section. When the current spikes, the relay tells a circuit breaker to open. Relays are nested: the one nearest the fault should act first, and the ones further upstream should wait and only step in if the near one fails.

Getting that ordering right is called coordination. It is the single most important thing a protection engineer does, and it is what half this project automates.

2. An arc flash is an electrical explosion

When a fault arcs inside a metal cabinet, the air itself becomes a conductor and burns at thousands of degrees. A technician standing in front of that panel takes the heat directly.

The damage is measured in calories per square centimetre (cal/cm²) landing on the person. Roughly: below 1.2 you are fine in ordinary clothes; around 8 you need serious protective gear; above 40 the industry position is that nobody should be working on it live at all.

Here is the part that matters: that number depends almost entirely on how long the arc burns. Twice the time, twice the energy. And what decides the time? The relay setting.

3. A loose connection is a fault waiting to happen

Bolted joints inside switchgear work loose over years. A loose joint has more electrical resistance, and resistance under load makes heat. So a connection that is slowly failing gets slowly hotter, long before it actually faults.

Put a temperature sensor on it and you can see the failure coming. That is what Schneider's ArcBlok product does, and it is what the second half of this project simulates.

The one idea that makes this a single product

Memorise this — it is the whole pitch

Arc-flash energy is set by how long the arc burns. How long it burns is set by the relay settings. So relay coordination and worker safety are not two problems — they are one loop, and today nobody closes it.

This is the answer when a judge asks "why is this one product instead of two projects glued together?" The thermal sensors tell you where a fault is about to be born. The coordination engine decides how fast it gets cleared once it is. Multiply those and you get a live danger level — replacing a sticker calculated five years ago.

The chain, end to end

Five steps, each one a file

  1. Describe the substation

    Build a model of the wiring, then calculate two things: how much current flows normally, and how much would flow during a short circuit at each location.

  2. Choose the relay settings

    Pick three numbers for every relay so that the nearest one always acts first, and the total time spent in a fault is as small as possible.

  3. Turn time into danger

    Take the resulting clearing times and compute how much energy would hit a person standing there, then convert that to a protective-gear rating.

  4. Watch the connections

    Stream temperatures from sensors on the joints and flag any one that is heating up relative to its neighbours.

  5. Show an operator

    Push all of it to a screen, live, with the arithmetic visible so an engineer can check the machine's reasoning instead of trusting it.

What each file does

In plain terms

grid.py

Owner: Bibek

The fake substation. Builds a model with a high-voltage supply, a transformer stepping 20,000 V down, three outgoing feeder lines, and a low-voltage board at the end of the first one.

Then it answers: how much current flows normally (59 A per feeder), and how much during a short circuit at each point (6,204 A at the main busbar, dropping to 4,592 A at the far end — it drops with distance because cable resists the flow).

That drop is the only clue a relay has about where the fault is.

coord.py

Owner: Bibek

Chooses the relay settings. Each relay has three knobs: how much current wakes it up, how long it waits, and how sharply its waiting time shrinks as current climbs.

It picks all three for all seven relays, then checks the answer against every fault location under both strong and weak supply conditions — settings that work at full strength can quietly fail when the grid is running light.

The third knob is where our advantage comes from. See below.

arcflash.py

Owner: Bibek

Turns clearing time into a danger rating. Takes the times from coord.py and computes the energy hitting a worker, then maps it to a protective gear category.

It also models a dedicated arc-flash detector — a device that senses the light of an arc and trips in milliseconds. That fixes a problem the relay settings alone cannot.

sensors.py

Owner: Vishal

Fake thermal sensors, and the logic that reads them. Six sensors on connection points, with one that can be made to slowly overheat on command.

The clever part: it does not use a fixed temperature limit. It compares each connection against its two neighbours, because room temperature and load swing everything by tens of degrees without anything being wrong.

api.py

Owner: Vishal

The glue. Runs a clock, holds the current state, and pushes updates to the browser continuously. Every other file stays independent — they only meet here.

It also serves the dashboard itself, so starting one command gives you the whole system.

static/index.html

Owner: Rudresh

The screen. One file: the diagram, the charts, the alerts, the buttons. No build step and nothing to install, which means one less thing that can fail on stage.

Why our settings beat a careful engineer's

A relay's third knob is its curve shape — how aggressively its reaction time drops as current rises. There are three standard shapes. A human doing this by hand picks one shape and uses it everywhere, because mixing them means redoing all the arithmetic by hand.

Across seven relays there are over two thousand combinations. A computer checks them in fifteen seconds. That is the entire source of our advantage, and it is worth being honest about: it is not magic, it is a search a human has no time to do.

Note the trap in row one: copy-paste settings look fastest. They are only fast because they are wrong — the wrong breaker opens. We compare against the careful engineer, not against the broken option, because beating a broken solution proves nothing.

Reading the dashboard

Panel by panel, left to right

Single-line diagram (the big picture)

This is the substation drawn as electricians draw it. Power enters at the top, passes through a transformer (the two overlapping circles), reaches the blue horizontal MV busbar, and splits into three feeders going down. The first feeder ends at a second transformer feeding the LV switchboard at the bottom.

The small squares are relays, each labelled with how fast it would act. Coloured dots are the temperature sensors — green is normal, amber is worth investigating, red needs immediate attention. When you inject a fault, relays that tripped turn red and the sections that lost power go grey.

Time-current curves

Seven curved lines, one per relay. Read it as: "at this much current (across), I react in this much time (down)". Curves sloping down to the right mean bigger fault, faster reaction.

What matters is the vertical gap between curves. Gap means the lower relay acts first and the upper one waits — correct coordination. Curves that touch or cross mean trouble.

Connection temperatures

Six wiggly lines, one per sensor. Normally they move together, because they all respond to the same room temperature and the same load. One line separating from the pack is the whole point — that is a joint going bad, and it is visible long before it reaches any alarming absolute temperature.

Arc-flash hazard (right side)

Two cards, one per place a person might stand. Each shows the energy in cal/cm², the protective gear category, how long the fault would burn, and the arc-flash boundary — how far back you would have to stand to be safe.

Coordination, Alerts, Mode comparison

Coordination says whether the current settings are valid, and lists any pair of relays that would fight each other. Alerts is the live feed of overheating connections. Mode comparison fills in as you switch between settings modes, so the audience can see the three approaches side by side.

The arc-flash device, and why it is a strong move

The MV busbar sits at 48 cal/cm² — well past the "do not work on this live" line — and optimising the settings only improved it by 8%. That is not a tuning failure; it is structural. The upstream relay must wait for the downstream ones to stay coordinated, and the waiting is what burns the technician.

A light-sensing arc-flash relay escapes this entirely, because an arc inside a cabinet is never a downstream fault — so that device needs no coordination at all and can trip instantly.

The demo, click by click

This sequence is your pitch — rehearse until it is automatic

  1. Press Copy-paste, then Inject fault

    Two relays trip instead of one. Three sections go dark when only one needed to. Say: "this is what stale settings do."

  2. Press Hand-graded, then Inject fault again

    Only the correct breaker opens now. Say: "a careful engineer fixes this in about a day of spreadsheet work."

  3. Press Optimise

    Fifteen seconds. Same safety, 17% faster, and the low-voltage board's danger rating halves. Say: "fifteen seconds, and it also checks conditions the manual method skips."

  4. Press Inject hotspot and keep talking

    After about twenty seconds one temperature line separates and alerts climb from investigate to repair to immediate. Say: "this joint is failing, and we knew hours before any fixed-threshold alarm would have noticed."

    The medium-voltage hazard collapses from prohibited to Category 2 with coordination untouched. Say: "and where settings alone cannot help, we can say exactly what hardware will."

What is real and what is simulated

Answer this honestly — the question is coming

Genuinely real

  • The power-system maths — fault currents follow the international standard method
  • The relay equations, straight from the IEC standard
  • The arc-flash energy model, from the IEEE standard
  • The protective-gear categories, from the NFPA safety code
  • The temperature severity bands, from industry inspection criteria
  • The optimisation, and its measured improvement

Simulated

  • The substation itself is a realistic model, not a real site
  • Temperature readings come from a physics-based simulator, not hardware
  • No real relay is being reprogrammed — we compute settings, we do not push them

This split is a strength, not a weakness. Say it plainly and move on: real standards, real maths, simulated site.

One correction to your submission

The form says IEEE 1584-2018. We use the 2002 edition of that standard plus a second method for high voltage. The 2018 version needs large data tables that only come with the purchased standard, and inventing numbers behind a safety claim is not acceptable. Either buy the standard through your faculty mentor, or say "IEEE 1584" without the year.

What is still missing

Two gaps, one urgent

Nobody has checked our assumptions. Seven numbers underpin every result — the required waiting gap between relays, the current margin, the relay's minimum reaction time, the breaker opening time, and three temperature thresholds. A faculty member can validate all seven in ten minutes. Until then, every figure on the screen is provisional.

There is no business case yet. Financial impact is a quarter of your final score and currently has nothing behind it. It needs five sourced figures: the cost of an hour of unplanned outage, the cost of an arc-flash injury, the engineer-hours in one manual coordination study, the cost of a periodic safety audit, and how many substations a utility runs. Real sources with links — an invented number dies to the first follow-up question.

Run it yourself: .venv\Scripts\python -m uvicorn api:app then open http://127.0.0.1:8000. Every file also self-checks — running python coord.py or python arcflash.py prints its own evidence and fails loudly if the logic breaks.
